Troubleshooting: something broke on the board — where to look first

Power problems share one trait: "similar symptoms, many causes". An unstable output could be an unstable loop, thermal shutdown cycling, or just a long probe ground. This page starts with a symptom index, then lists "symptom → likely cause → how to check → fix" by category, and ends with design, process and test issues and debugging tips.

Rule one　First confirm the measurement is right, then confirm the operating conditions are within spec, and only then suspect the chip. More than half of power "failures" are solved in the first two steps.

Overheating and thermal shutdown

  • Symptom: the output drops and recovers every tens of ms to seconds, the package is too hot to touch, and it goes away with less load or lower VIN.
  • Calculate first: PD = (VIN − VOUT)·IOUT; estimate TJ with the real board's thermal resistance. Often one calculation shows it is over the limit.
  • Common causes:
    • VIN higher than designed (e.g. a 12 V adapter instead of 5 V).
    • Exposed pad not soldered, excessive voids (check by X-ray), too few thermal vias.
    • 2-layer board or cut-up copper; real θJA far above the datasheet.
    • Loop oscillation adding loss.
    • Shorted output or abnormal load: in current limit, all of VIN·ILIM is dissipated on the chip.
  • Fixes: lower VIN (pre-regulate), add a series input resistor to share dissipation, choose a lower-θ package, add copper and vias, or switch to DC-DC.

Efficiency below expectation

  • Measurement: is voltage four-wire measured at the IC pins? Are the meter shunt and lead drops included in PIN?
  • Inductor: DCR too high, saturation, AC loss; compare with the datasheet's recommended inductor.
  • Light load: is it in FPWM mode? (Poor light-load efficiency is then normal.)
  • Frequency: externally set higher than intended, increasing switching loss.
  • Body-diode conduction: dead time too long or an unsuitable external low-side MOSFET.
  • Heat: hot RDS(on) rises and efficiency falls — positive feedback.

Reverse current and leakage

  • Voltage remains after shutdown: another supply feeds this rail through I/O ESD diodes, or the load holds stored energy. Enable active discharge at shutdown, or avoid it in the system sequence.
  • LDO input off, output held by a battery or big capacitor: the PMOS body diode passes current from output to input, possibly damaging the LDO or upsetting the upstream rail. Use an LDO with reverse-current protection, or a series / ideal diode.
  • Standby current too high: floating EN half-enabling the part, pull-ups, PG pull-ups, divider leakage, hot leakage; disconnect one by one and measure.
  • Unpowered PMIC fed by I²C pull-ups: digital-pin ESD diodes half-power the PMIC, which may get stuck in an unknown state and fail to start properly later.

EMI failures

  • Conducted EMI (150 kHz–108 MHz): mainly the switching frequency and harmonics leaving through the input. Bigger input filter, spread spectrum, a different frequency.
  • Radiated EMI (above 30 MHz): hot-loop area and SW ringing. Shrink the hot loop, add small (0402 / 0201) input capacitors, a BOOT resistor or an RC snubber.
  • Near-field scanning finds hot spots far faster than trial and error in the chamber.
  • Cables and enclosure: long output leads are antennas too; common-mode chokes and grounding often help more than changing IC settings.

Measurement and debugging tips

  1. Confirm conditions: VIN (at the IC pins), load, temperature, EN and the actual external component values, compared with datasheet conditions.
  2. Measure correctly: ground spring or coax on the scope; 20 MHz bandwidth limit for ripple, full bandwidth for spikes; a current probe for inductor current.
  3. Watch several signals at once: VIN, VOUT, SW, IL, EN and PG together, to see what moves first.
  4. ABA swap: put the suspect IC on a good board and a good IC on the problem board; if the problem follows the IC it is the chip, if it stays with the board it is the application.
  5. Change one variable: capacitor, load, temperature — one at a time.
  6. Thermal camera: find abnormal hot spots, including neighbouring parts.
  7. Read fault registers: PMICs usually record the last shutdown cause.
  8. Preserve the evidence: do not keep powering a failed board to "fix" it; secondary damage spoils later failure analysis.

These are general industry practices, not tied to any company or product. Datasheets and vendor application notes are authoritative.
