Customer complaints

Customer complaints: from the customer's e-mail to a closed 8D

A complaint usually starts with a short e-mail: "Twelve of your LDOs failed on our line." Over the following days or weeks, quality, product, test, design and applications engineers must answer three questions together: Is it our problem? How big is the exposure? How do we make sure it never happens again? This page covers the handling flow, failure-analysis steps, the 8D report and the most typical power-IC complaint scenarios.

About the cases　The cases on this page are composites of common industry situations, written to show the reasoning. They do not describe any particular company or product.

Flow and timelines

  1. Acknowledge (within 24 hours): confirm receipt, open a complaint number, assign an owner.
  2. Initial containment (24–72 hours): decide whether to freeze inventory, screen goods in transit and notify other customers on the same lots.
  3. Gather information: failure rate, lot / date code, operating conditions, failure mode, photos and waveforms (see what to ask).
  4. Get samples: failing parts plus good parts from the same lot, and the failing board if possible. Ask the customer not to keep powering it to try fixes.
  5. Verify the failure: reproduce it on ATE and on the bench. If it cannot be reproduced it is NTF — go back to the application conditions.
  6. Failure analysis (FA): from non-destructive to destructive, locate the physical failure and its mechanism (see FA flow).
  7. Root cause and exposure: link the physical failure to a process, package, test, design or application root cause, and decide which lots are affected.
  8. Correct and prevent: short-term actions, permanent actions, horizontal deployment (do other products share the risk?).
  9. Close the 8D: verify the actions work and get the customer to agree to close.

Common time requirements: 3D (including containment) within 24–48 hours and the full 8D within 10–30 working days; automotive customers are usually stricter. FA time is the biggest variable — a complex on-die failure can take weeks.

"What changed recently?" is the most valuable question　When a product has run for a long time and suddenly starts failing, the answer is usually hidden in a change: the customer switched capacitor vendors, we changed a test program, a distributor topped up stock from the open market. Lining up the change points with when failures began often points the way.

The failure-analysis flow

  1. Visual inspection: optical microscope for package cracks, burns, damaged leads, solder residue and correct marking (counterfeit check).
  2. X-ray: broken or displaced bond wires, solder voids, die cracks.
  3. C-SAM acoustic scan: delamination and voids inside the package.
  4. Electrical verification: full ATE program and bench reproduction under customer conditions; compare with good parts from the same lot.
  5. Pin-to-pin I-V curves (curve trace): find which pins are shorted, leaky or open, compared with a good part.
  6. Decapsulation: remove mould compound chemically or by laser to expose the die.
  7. Hot-spot localisation: EMMI (light emission), OBIRCH (laser-induced resistance change), lock-in thermography to find leakage or shorts.
  8. Deprocessing and inspection: delayering, SEM, FIB cross-sections and EDX composition to confirm the physical defect.
  9. Conclusion: failure site, mechanism and likely root cause, with photos and data.

The rule is non-destructive first, destructive later: once decapsulated or delayered, you cannot go back. Photograph everything before each step.

Among returned power ICs, EOS and NTF are usually the two largest classes. Genuine chip defects are a small share, but each one must be taken seriously because it may signal a whole-lot risk.

The escape root cause often matters more　Defects will always occasionally happen — but why did test miss it? For a UVLO fault that only appears when cold, the occurrence cause is a process shift and the escape cause is room-temperature-only FT. Fixing the process without adding the test lets the next kind of shift escape too.

Root-cause tools

  • 5 Whys: keep asking "why" until you reach a system cause you can change, not "operator error".
  • Fishbone (Ishikawa): list possible causes by man, machine, material, method, environment and measurement (6M) so nothing is missed.
  • Is / is-not analysis: which lots, customers and conditions have the problem — and which do not? The differences are the clues.
  • Fault-tree analysis (FTA): expand every possible path below the top failure event and eliminate them one by one with data.
  • Data analysis: map failing units back to CP / FT data to see where they sat in the distribution; they often turn out to have been marginal or PAT outliers.

Typical complaint cases

EOSHot-plug spike destroys a buck inputConsumer · 12 V adapter
Symptom
Products occasionally fail to power on at end users; returned buck ICs have VIN shorted to ground, at about 0.1% failure rate.
Analysis
FA shows a large melted area in the high-side switch — classic EOS. Plugging a 12 V adapter with a long cable into the customer board shows a 23 V spike on VIN, above the IC's 20 V absolute maximum.
Root cause
The input has only ceramic capacitors, forming a lightly damped LC with the adapter cable inductance; the new design dropped the original electrolytic to save cost.
Action
Add a TVS and an electrolytic for damping at the input; longer term, move to a 28 V-rated part. Add hot-plug guidance to the application note.
Lesson
EOS is a starting point, not a conclusion; find where the energy came from and reproduce it on the customer's board.
ApplicationNew capacitor supplier, and the LDO starts oscillatingIndustrial · 3.3 V LDO
Symptom
After two trouble-free years in production, 3% of boards in one month show bad ADC readings in functional test; the customer suspects an LDO lot problem.
Analysis
All returned LDOs pass (NTF). The failing boards show 400 kHz oscillation on the LDO output. ABA swap: a good IC on a bad board still oscillates.
Root cause
Purchasing switched to a different 0402, 6.3 V, 2.2 µF MLCC that keeps only about 0.8 µF at 3.3 V bias — below the LDO's 1 µF minimum. With capacitor tolerance some boards oscillate.
Action
Move to a 0603, 10 V-rated capacitor; the customer's BOM adds the capacitor DC-bias spec to its qualification criteria.
Lesson
An "IC lot problem" is often a "lot problem in another part"; ask proactively about the customer's changes.
ProductionWrong OTP setting, wrong output voltagePMIC · part configuration
Symptom
A customer receives a reel of PMICs where one rail outputs 1.8 V instead of 1.2 V, damaging the SoC.
Analysis
Reading out the OTP shows the settings of another part number built on the same die. Traced to an FT part-number changeover where the test program switched but the programming file was not updated.
Root cause
Occurrence: part-number changeover relied on manual checks. Escape: FT only verified that programming "succeeded", not that the content matched the part number.
Action
Test programs load the programming file automatically by part number; after programming, read back the OTP and compare a CRC with the part master; recall and screen all lots from the same period.
Lesson
The risk in configurable chips lies in configuration management; verify the content after programming, not just the action.
TestCold UVLO threshold escapes testAutomotive · LDO
Symptom
On winter cold starts, ECUs in a few vehicles reset. Returned LDOs work at room temperature.
Analysis
At −40 °C, the failing units' rising UVLO threshold is 150 mV above spec; others from the lot are fine. CP data shows the failing units were already extreme values at room temperature.
Root cause
Occurrence: a local wafer process shift gave the UVLO comparator an abnormal temperature coefficient. Escape: FT tested only room and hot, and UVLO was not included in PAT.
Action
Add a cold test, or apply PAT dynamic limits to the room-temperature value to remove outliers; review test-temperature coverage for other temperature-sensitive parameters.
Lesson
Outliers often signal latent defects; choose test temperatures by each parameter's temperature behaviour, not one size fits all.
PackageMSL violation causes delaminationConsumer · QFN PMIC
Symptom
Post-SMT functional-test fallout suddenly rises to 0.5%, with output shifts and intermittent opens.
Analysis
C-SAM shows delamination between die surface and mould compound; unmounted parts from the same lot are fine. The reel had been open on the line for over two weeks.
Root cause
An MSL 3 part was used beyond its floor life (168 hours) without baking; absorbed moisture vaporised in reflow, delaminating, pulling on bond wires and changing die stress.
Action
The customer introduces moisture-sensitive device control (open-time logging, dry cabinets, bake when expired). The supplier strengthens the warning on the packaging label.
Lesson
Not every failure happens inside the chipmaker; the customer's process control is part of reliability too.
NTFAll returns are good: a soldering problemWearable · WLCSP LDO
Symptom
After drop testing, some products show output faults; the customer returns 20 LDOs for analysis.
Analysis
All 20 pass ATE and bench tests. A failing board is requested; a cross-section shows cracks between the WLCSP solder balls and PCB pads.
Root cause
PCB pad design and underfill did not follow recommendations, concentrating drop stress in the joints.
Action
Fix the pad size per recommendation and add underfill; the standard NTF process now requires the failing board.
Lesson
NTF does not mean "no problem"; removing the IC may destroy the evidence.
ChangeAn unannounced mould-compound changeIndustrial · precision LDO
Symptom
In the customer's hot-humid ageing test, LDO output drifts 0.8%, beyond the system's 0.5% allowance; older lots do not.
Analysis
Comparing lots shows the assembly site switched to an equivalent-grade mould compound for supply reasons, judged internally as "no spec impact" and issued no PCN. The new compound changes stress more after absorbing moisture.
Root cause
The change-impact assessment looked only at room-temperature electricals, not humidity stress on precision parameters.
Action
Revert to the original compound; change-control rules now require post-humidity drift qualification and customer notification for any material change that affects die stress.
Lesson
"No spec impact" must be proven with qualification data; when in doubt, issue a PCN.
ESDSystem ESD testing kills the ICConsumer · USB port power switch
Symptom
After IEC 61000-4-2 ±8 kV contact discharge, the load switch fails; the customer says the datasheet's ±2 kV HBM is not enough.
Analysis
FA shows the input ESD structure melted. The IC meets its HBM and CDM ratings; system-level discharge carries far more energy and current than HBM.
Root cause
No external TVS on the USB port, so all the system ESD energy entered the IC.
Action
Add a TVS at the port, close to the connector; the application note explains the difference between HBM and system-level ESD.
Lesson
Component-level and system-level ESD are different specifications; pins connected to the outside world need system protection.
CounterfeitCounterfeits bought on the open marketDuring a shortage · LDO
Symptom
During a shortage the customer buys from an unauthorised channel; the new lot has high IQ, large dropout and some units are unstable.
Analysis
Marking font differs from the original, date and lot codes do not match the manufacturer's records; after decap the die marking belongs to another vendor's low-end product.
Root cause
Remarked counterfeits from an unauthorised channel.
Action
The customer buys only from the manufacturer or authorised distributors; the manufacturer offers a lot-verification service.
Lesson
Confirming the purchase channel is one of the first steps; parts not shipped by the manufacturer are not its quality problem, but help the customer identify them anyway.
DesignFoldback current limit stops an LDO startingIndustrial · FPGA auxiliary rail
Symptom
On some boards the output sticks at 0.6 V after power-up instead of reaching 2.5 V; re-powering sometimes helps.
Analysis
The FPGA auxiliary rail starts drawing heavily at low voltage; the LDO's foldback allows only 200 mA at low VOUT, not enough to supply the load and charge the 100 µF output capacitor.
Root cause
The application is incompatible with foldback limiting; the datasheet shows the foldback curve but the application section gives no warning.
Action
Switch to a constant-current-limit part, or delay the load with PG; add start-up guidance to the datasheet application section.
Lesson
Protection behaviour is part of the specification; spell out when it will "over-protect".

Talking to the customer

  • Respond first, analyse second: an initial reply within 24 hours stating receipt, next steps and timeline beats a full report after three days of silence.
  • Let data speak: "the IC is fine" needs test data, swap results and waveforms, not just a conclusion.
  • Do not rush to assign blame: an EOS or application conclusion still needs a concrete energy source or condition, and improvement suggestions. Customers want a solution, not a verdict.
  • Interim reports: for long FAs, update progress every few days.
  • One contact: a single point of contact keeps departments from giving conflicting answers.
  • Follow up after closing: confirm the actions work at the customer and the failure rate returns to baseline.
