Chapter 3 · Circuit design

Design: stable, quiet and able to protect itself

Most of a power IC's design time goes into three things: keeping the loop stable for every load, capacitor and temperature; making the output quiet and fast; and protecting the chip and its load from shorts, overheating, reverse current and static. This chapter starts with the LDO loop and moves on to DC-DC control and the digital side of a PMIC.

The LDO loop: poles, zeros and phase margin

Treat the LDO as a negative-feedback amplifier. Open-loop gain AOL = AEA · gm,pass · Rout · β, where β = R2/(R1+R2). Stability is decided by where a few poles and zeros sit:

Pole / zeroLocationBehaviour
Output pole pout1 / (2π · Rout · COUT), Rout = ro,pass ∥ RloadMoves with load: at heavy load Rout falls and the pole moves up by tens of times
Gate pole pgate1 / (2π · Ro,EA · Cgate)The bigger the pass device, the bigger Cgate and the lower the pole
ESR zero zESR1 / (2π · ESR · COUT)Set by the external capacitor; can restore phase, or lift gain at high frequency
Parasitic polesEA internal nodes, capacitor ESL, tracesUsually above a few MHz

A classic PMOS LDO has high output impedance, so the dominant pole is at the output and the second pole at the gate. If the two are too close, phase has turned nearly 180° by unity gain and the loop oscillates. The usual target is phase margin ≥ 45° under all conditions (60° for conservative designs) and gain margin ≥ 10 dB.

The ESR window and ceramic capacitors

Early LDOs were designed around tantalum or aluminium electrolytic capacitors with 0.1 to several Ω of ESR, which supplied exactly the zero needed for stability. Datasheets drew a "stable region of ESR vs load current", nicknamed the tunnel of death: too little ESR (ceramic) oscillates, and so does too much.

Once multilayer ceramic capacitors (MLCCs) took over, with only a few mΩ of ESR, old LDOs simply oscillated with them. Modern LDOs are internally compensated — Miller compensation or an internal zero in the EA so the dominant pole no longer depends on ESR — and are marked "stable with ceramic capacitors". Even so:

  • Minimum effective capacitance is still a hard limit. Under DC bias an MLCC can drop to 30–50% of nominal (see Applications · capacitors); the customer thinks they fitted 1 µF but actually have 0.4 µF.
  • Too much capacitance can also cause trouble: the output pole drops too low, and start-up inrush can trip the current limit. Datasheets often give a maximum COUT.
  • Trace inductance and ESL: a capacitor placed far from the IC looks smaller at high frequency and resonates, turning phase sharply around a few MHz.

Capacitor-less LDOs and NMOS LDOs

Digital LDOs inside an SoC or other on-chip LDOs have no room for µF capacitors — only hundreds of pF to a few nF on die. The output pole moves to high frequency and the dominant pole is put inside the EA (Miller compensation), at the cost of limited bandwidth and large undershoot in load transients. Common remedies:

  • Flipped voltage follower (FVF) and local fast loops: a fast local loop at the output handles high-frequency transients.
  • Capacitively coupled transient boost: sense output dV/dt and push the pass-device gate directly.
  • Digital LDO (DLDO): digitally controlled switch arrays replace the analogue EA and work at very low voltage, but suffer limit cycles and quantisation ripple.
  • NMOS source follower: output impedance ≈ 1/gm puts the output pole naturally high, so stability and transient are both good; the gate must be VGS above VOUT, needing another bias rail or an internal charge pump.

PSRR: keeping input ripple out

PSRR = 20·log(ΔVIN/ΔVOUT). It is not one number but a curve against frequency, roughly in three regions:

RegionDominant mechanismHow to improve
Low frequency (< 1/10 of loop bandwidth)Loop gain: PSRR ≈ AOLMore EA gain, a better-rejecting reference, reference low-pass filtering
Mid frequency (near loop bandwidth)Loop gain falls with frequency, so PSRR worsens; the worst point is usually hereMore bandwidth; gate drive that tracks VIN (feed-forward cancellation)
High frequency (> a few MHz)No loop gain left; COUT divides against parasitics; PMOS Cgs couples the input straight throughLarge COUT, low ESL, NMOS pass device, series RC filtering
  • Headroom: with too little VIN − VOUT the pass device enters the linear region and PSRR can drop 20–40 dB. A post-regulating LDO after a buck needs 200–500 mV of headroom.
  • Load current: at heavy load the pass-device ro falls and low-frequency PSRR usually worsens.
  • DC-DC post-regulation: with a 2 MHz buck, look at the LDO's PSRR at 2 MHz and its harmonics, not the 1 kHz number.

Output noise

Most LDO output noise comes from the reference and the EA input stage, then is amplified by the divider's (1 + R1/R2). So the same LDO is noisier at 3.3 V than at 1.2 V.

  • NR / SS pin: an external capacitor after the reference forms an RC low-pass that filters reference noise; it doubles as soft start — the larger it is, the slower the start.
  • Feed-forward capacitor CFF: across R1, it makes the feedback gain 1 at high frequency so noise is no longer amplified; it also adds a pole-zero pair that can improve transient response but can change phase margin.
  • 1/f noise: low-frequency noise is dominated by MOS flicker noise; BJT or large-area MOS input stages are better. Chopping removes it but adds ripple at the chopping frequency.
  • Who cares: PLLs, VCOs, high-resolution ADCs, image sensors and audio; < 10 µVRMS integrated from 10 Hz to 100 kHz is a common requirement.

Load transient

When load current jumps from 1 mA to 500 mA in 1 µs, the loop cannot react in time and the output capacitor supplies all of it:

ΔVOUT ≈ ΔI · ESR + ΔI · tresp / COUT　　tresp ≈ 1/(2π·fUGB) + Cgate·ΔVgate/Islew
  • Loop bandwidth: higher is faster, but fights stability.
  • Gate slew rate: a big pass device can have hundreds of pF of gate capacitance; if the EA cannot source enough current the gate takes a long time to move. Common fixes are a buffer between EA and pass device, or adaptive biasing (raising bias at heavy load or on a detected transient).
  • Light-to-heavy is the hardest: at light load IQ is low, gate drive is weak and the output pole is low — the worst transient case. Low IQ versus fast transient is the classic LDO trade-off.
  • Overshoot: when a heavy load is suddenly removed the pass device cannot shut off instantly and the extra current charges COUT; some LDOs add active discharge or pull-down to absorb it.

Bandgap references

A BJT's VBE is about 0.6–0.7 V with a tempco of about −2 mV/°C (CTAT); the ΔVBE of two BJTs at different current densities, (kT/q)·ln(N), is proportional to absolute temperature (PTAT). Adding them in the right ratio cancels the tempco near one temperature; the sum is about 1.2 V, close to silicon's bandgap.

VREF = VBE + K · (kT/q)·ln(N) ≈ 1.2 V　(K set by a resistor ratio)
  • Curvature: VBE is not perfectly linear; after first-order compensation a parabolic residue remains, about 10–30 ppm/°C over temperature. Higher-order compensation reduces it further.
  • Sub-1 V bandgaps: low-voltage systems use current mode — sum CTAT and PTAT currents into a resistor — so the output can be any value (e.g. 0.5 V).
  • Error sources: op-amp offset (amplified by the ln-term gain), resistor ratio, BJT area ratio, package stress, substrate injection. Offset is the largest, which is why bandgaps usually need trimming (see Process · trimming).
  • Start-up circuit: a bandgap has a stable "all currents zero" state and needs a start-up circuit to escape it. A marginal start-up circuit causes "occasional no output" — very hard to reproduce.

Compensation networks

  • Crossover frequency: usually fc ≤ fSW/10 (up to fSW/5 for current mode); boost / buck-boost also below 1/3–1/5 of the RHPZ.
  • Type II (one zero, two poles): common with current mode; zero near the power-stage dominant pole, high-frequency pole at the ESR zero or fSW/2.
  • Type III (two zeros, three poles): voltage mode, to recover the 180° phase swing of the LC double pole.
  • Internal compensation: many integrated bucks compensate on-chip for simplicity, but that restricts the usable L and C; customers using the wrong parts may see instability.
  • Measuring it: in the lab, inject into the feedback path (e.g. a 10–50 Ω injection resistor) with a network analyser, or estimate phase margin from the ringing in a load-transient waveform (see Test · characterisation).

Gate drive, dead time and bootstrap

  • Bootstrap: an N-type high-side switch needs its gate VGS above SW; a capacitor charges while the low side is on and "floats" on SW when the high side is on. At 100% duty it cannot recharge, so the low side must be forced on periodically or a charge pump added.
  • Dead time: prevents shoot-through. Too long means more body-diode conduction, more loss and worse substrate injection; too short risks shoot-through. Adaptive dead time waits until the other gate is confirmed off.
  • Switching speed vs EMI: faster SW dV/dt lowers switching loss but raises ringing and EMI. Use segmented or adjustable drive, or a resistor in series with BOOT (a common application trick).
  • Minimum on / off time: tON,min limits the maximum step-down ratio (2 MHz with 60 ns tON,min gives Dmin = 12%, so 12 V can only go down to about 1.4 V); beyond that the converter skips pulses.

The digital side of a PMIC

  • Power-up / power-down sequencer: enables each rail in the order and with the delays set in OTP, waiting for the previous rail's PG. Power-down order matters just as much; getting it wrong can damage the processor or cause latch-up.
  • Interfaces: I²C, SPI, SPMI (mobile), PMBus (servers), SVID / SVI3 (x86 processors), for reading and writing voltages, modes, fault status and telemetry.
  • DVS: on a new voltage command the reference slews at a set rate (e.g. 10 mV/µs) and the loop must stay stable throughout; when stepping down, a converter that cannot sink current (PFM) lags the command.
  • Fault state machine: on OCP, OVP, UVP or thermal warning, decide whether to turn off just that rail, everything, hiccup or latch off until reset — and record the cause in a register for later analysis.
  • OTP and part numbers: default voltages, sequences, protection thresholds and I²C address live in OTP. Design in OTP read-back checks (CRC / parity) and a post-program verification flow.
  • Functional safety (automotive): an independent second reference and monitor comparators, watchdog, built-in self-test (BIST) and fault-injection paths to meet ISO 26262 ASIL levels.

Glossary

Phase margin (PM)
How far the phase is from −180° where the gain falls to 0 dB.
UGB
Unity-gain bandwidth: the frequency where loop gain is 1, i.e. crossover.
ESR / ESL
A capacitor's equivalent series resistance / inductance.
Miller compensation
A capacitor across a gain stage that uses the Miller effect to move the dominant pole down and split the poles.
CFF
Feed-forward capacitor across the upper divider resistor.
Foldback
Current limit that falls as output voltage falls.
Hiccup
On a fault, shut down for a while and then retry.
Slope compensation
A ramp that prevents subharmonic oscillation in peak current mode above 50% duty.
COT
Constant on-time control.
PTAT / CTAT
Voltage or current proportional / complementary to absolute temperature.
SPMI
System Power Management Interface, the MIPI bus for mobile power management.
FMEDA
Failure Modes, Effects and Diagnostic Analysis, a functional-safety method.
