Chapter 5 · Test & qualification

Test: you only know it is good if you measure it right

Power-IC testing comes in three kinds: screening bad parts on ATE in the shortest possible time; measuring every parameter under every condition on the bench (characterisation); and proving with accelerated stress that the part will outlive the product (qualification). The three sets of numbers must agree, or nobody can convince anybody when a complaint arrives.

DC-DC parameters

  • Efficiency: η = POUT/PIN, with four-wire voltage sensing, precision shunts and thermal equilibrium at each load point. A 1% measurement error is enough to make efficiency curves "look" different.
  • RDS(on): hold the high or low side on in a test mode, force a known current and Kelvin-measure the drop.
  • Switching frequency and minimum on-time: measure SW; production can use a divided-down output of the internal oscillator instead of a large inductor.
  • Peak / valley current limit: cycle-by-cycle thresholds, needing a real inductor or a test mode.
  • Light-load mode threshold, ripple and load transient: mostly characterisation items; production only checks function.

ATE production test

A power-IC ATE (e.g. a mixed-signal tester) has several DPS (device power supplies), PMUs (precision measurement units), high-current / high-voltage sources, digital channels and digitisers. The core challenge of production test is measuring every guaranteed parameter in 0.5 s without giving up accuracy.

  • Settling time: with a large output capacitor, each change of condition needs ms to tens of ms. Production load boards use smaller capacitors or relay switching.
  • Test modes: a special pin sequence or I²C command enters test mode, routing internal nodes (reference, bias current, oscillator) to an analogue mux for direct measurement, and can disable soft start or protection to save time.
  • Multi-site: 4–32 sites in parallel; shared resources (such as precision digitisers) reduce parallel efficiency.
  • Load-board design: parasitic resistance and inductance of high-current paths, relay life and thermal design all affect measurement. Load boards need their own periodic verification and maintenance.
  • Test-program version control: every change to limits, items or algorithms is recorded and correlation-checked; automotive products also notify customers.

Design for test (DFT)

  • Analogue test bus: key internal nodes are muxed to a test pin or a functional pin.
  • "Trial trim" in registers: write the trim code to a register first (without burning), measure, and only then program OTP — fewer programming errors.
  • Protection bypass: test mode temporarily disables UVLO, soft start or thermal-shutdown delays.
  • Digital scan chains: the PMIC sequencer and registers are scan-tested, targeting > 95% coverage.
  • Test-mode safety: it must not be entered by noise or a stray I²C command in the customer's system. Products have entered test mode from pin noise at power-up, causing abnormal outputs in the field.

Kelvin sensing and high current

When testing a 30 mΩ switch, socket contact resistance alone may be 20–50 mΩ and rises with use. The answer is Kelvin (four-wire) measurement: one pair of force wires carries current, another pair of sense wires measures voltage at the pins; with almost no current in the sense wires, contact resistance does not affect the reading.

  • Socket wear: contact resistance creeps up and RDS(on) or load-regulation distributions shift until yield drops or testers disagree. Check with a golden unit every shift and set replacement counts.
  • Pulsed test: high-current tests use 0.1–1 ms pulses to avoid self-heating, and reduce socket arcing and burning.
  • In-package resistance: measured RDS(on) includes bond wires and lead frame. Datasheets usually quote "including package"; the test must state the measurement point.

Correlation and GR&R

The same chip measured at CP, FT, on the bench and on the customer's line will not read exactly the same. Correlation proves that the differences are known and acceptable.

  • Golden units: a set of samples precisely measured on the bench, used to verify every tester and load board.
  • GR&R (measurement-system analysis): repeatability (same tester) and reproducibility (different testers / operators) as a share of tolerance; < 10% good, 10–30% conditional, > 30% unacceptable.
  • Tester to tester: offsets between testers must be a small part of the guard band, or one tester's yield will be systematically low or high.
  • CP vs FT: pair each die's CP and FT data to compute package shift and recalibrate the CP trim target (see Production · CP trim).
  • With customers: when a customer's line reads "out of spec", first compare methods — load, temperature, measurement point and timing versus the datasheet conditions.

The probe-ground lesson　Measuring buck output ripple with a scope's long ground lead lets the loop pick up the SW node's magnetic field and shows spikes of hundreds of mV that are not real ripple. Use a ground spring, coax, or 50 Ω termination straight across the capacitor. Many "ripple too high" complaints turn out to be measurement problems.

Reliability qualification: AEC-Q100

The automotive IC standard is AEC-Q100; consumer and industrial products are often based on JEDEC JESD47. The typical requirement is 3 lots × 77 units, zero failures, with pre/post parameter comparison.

  • Temperature grades: Grade 0 (−40 to 150 °C), Grade 1 (−40 to 125 °C), Grade 2 (−40 to 105 °C), Grade 3 (−40 to 85 °C).
  • Acceleration models: Arrhenius (thermal, Ea about 0.7 eV), Peck (humidity), Coffin-Manson (temperature cycling) convert stress time to field life.
  • Parametric drift: beyond zero failures, pre/post changes must be within limits, e.g. output-voltage drift < 0.5%.
  • Power-IC specific items: short-circuit robustness (AEC-Q100-012, repeated shorts on smart power devices), SOA verification, high-voltage stress.

ESD and latch-up

ModelSimulatesTypical level
HBM (human body model)A charged person touching a pin, 100 pF / 1.5 kΩ±2 kV (general), ±4 kV (common in automotive)
CDM (charged device model)The charged chip itself touching ground through one pin; ns-scale high current±500 V, corner pins ±750 V
System-level IEC 61000-4-2A person touching the finished product; far more energy than HBM±8 kV contact / ±15 kV air (product level)
  • Passing HBM ≠ passing system level: an IC's ±2 kV HBM does not mean it survives ±8 kV on a USB or battery terminal; the system needs an external TVS. Customers often confuse the two, leading to "static killed the IC" complaints.
  • ESD challenges in power ICs: high-voltage pins cannot use ordinary clamps (the trigger must exceed the operating voltage), and LDMOS snapback must be handled carefully or the device burns locally during ESD.
  • Latch-up: a parasitic PNPN structure, once triggered by injected current, conducts until power is removed. Switching nodes and substrate injection make power ICs especially susceptible (see Process · substrate injection).

Functional-safety testing

  • Power-on self-test: monitor comparators, references and watchdog check themselves at every power-up; failure blocks release.
  • Fault injection: switches designed into the monitor paths let production and the field verify that the monitors themselves work (latent faults).
  • ATE coverage: functional safety requires production test to cover the safety mechanisms themselves, or hardware defects in them go unnoticed.
