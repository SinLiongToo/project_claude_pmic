Automotive smart high-side switches: the BTS50025-1TEA

The BTS50025-1TEA is often called "an Infineon power MOSFET", but it is really a smart high-side switch (Infineon's product name is Power PROFET™ + 12 V). Inside is a 2.5 mΩ-class N-channel power MOSFET plus a charge pump, gate driver, current sense, overcurrent / overtemperature / over-power protection, reverse-battery protection and an inductive-load clamp, all in one TO-252 package. Its job is to replace relays and fuses in the car and drive loads of tens of amps directly: heaters, glow plugs, fans and pumps. This page explains how such parts work, how to use them, how they are tested, and where they tend to cause trouble.

About the product information　Values on this page come from Infineon's public BTS50025-1TEA datasheet Rev.1.1 (2019-09-30) and product page (see Sources), compiled in October 2026. Most figures are typical values or values under specific conditions; design with the full conditions and curves in the latest datasheet. This page does not represent Infineon.

It is not just a MOSFET

A discrete power MOSFET has only gate, drain and source; drive, protection and diagnostics all live outside. A smart switch integrates them with the power device, usually in one smart-power process, or with a control die and a power die stacked in one package.

Ideas from the rest of this site apply directly: the power device is a DMOS; a high-side NMOS needs a gate above the supply, the same problem as an NMOS LDO, solved the same way with a charge pump; overcurrent and overtemperature protection share their roots with LDO protection, except that here the device latches instead of restarting by itself.

Why high side, and why replace relays

In a car one end of the load usually goes straight to the body (ground), so the switch sits between the battery and the load: the high side. The load is then unpowered when off, and a harness short to the body cannot switch the load on; the price is that an N-channel MOSFET needs a gate above the battery voltage.

Current sense: using the IS pin

Next to the power MOSFET sits a tiny "sense mirror" MOSFET sharing its drain and gate, with an area ratio of about 1 : 18 000, so about 1/18 000 of the load current flows through it. That current comes out of the IS pin and through a resistor RIS to ground, becoming a voltage for the ADC:

IIS = IL / dkILIS + IIS0　　VIS = IIS × RIS　　e.g. 20 A → 20 / 18 000 ≈ 1.1 mA → about 1.1 V on 1 kΩ
  • Accuracy: dkILIS spans 15 500–20 500, about ±14%; the offset is equivalent to about ±0.13 A of load current (25 °C). At high current the error is a percentage; at light load the offset dominates, and loads below 0.2 A are barely readable.
  • Two-point calibration: measure IIS at two known currents on the line or at start-up to get each part's own slope and offset; what remains is drift with temperature and ageing (about −3.5% to +2% from −40 °C to 150 °C relative to 25 °C).
  • Fault signal: after a short circuit or overtemperature latch, IS sources a fixed IIS(FAULT) (5–18 mA), far above the normal maximum. With 1 kΩ that is at least 5 V, so a full-scale ADC reading means a fault. This voltage exceeds a 3.3 V or 5 V ADC supply, so add a series RIS_PROT (e.g. 10 kΩ) to limit the current.
  • Headroom: the sense is linear only while VIS ≤ VOUT − 3.5 V. A larger RIS gives a bigger signal but saturates sooner at low battery voltage.
  • Timing: up to 1.5 ms to settle after turn-on (within ±10% in 700 µs max.), about 5 µs after a load change. Software should not sample right after turn-on, and in PWM should sample late in the on-time.
  • Open load: only detectable while on, as a current near zero; with the switch off IS gives no signal, and this part has no off-state open-load detection.

Protection: why it latches

Every protective turn-off on this part latches: the output stays off and IS sources the fault current until the controller releases IN (turns it off) for longer than tIN(RESETDELAY) (6 µs to 5 ms). Unlike the auto-restart thermal shutdown (hiccup) common in LDOs, this hands the decision of whether and how often to retry to software, so the device does not keep reheating itself into a short.

Retry strategy　Protection is for abnormal conditions; the datasheet states it is designed for neither continuous nor repetitive operation. Software retries must not exceed ffault = 200 Hz, and short-circuit endurance is limited to a number of events (qualified to AEC-Q100-012). In practice, limit the number of retries (stop and report a diagnostic code after a few) and lengthen the interval each time.

ReverSave: reverse battery

With the battery reversed, VS goes negative and current flows from ground through the load and the MOSFET's body diode back to the battery. At 20 A the body diode drops about 0.7 V, about 14 W, which a TO-252 cannot take for long.

ReverSave turns the MOSFET on during reverse battery so the current flows through the channel instead: RDS(REV) is 4 mΩ typ. (10 mΩ max. at 150 °C), about 1.6 W at 20 A. This requires a diode D across the external drive transistor so IN has a path during reverse battery. Note that:

  • ReverSave protects the switch itself; the load still sees the reverse voltage. Motors run backwards, and electronic loads need their own reverse protection.
  • The reverse current is set by the load; the datasheet tests with RL ≥ 0.68 Ω for up to 5 minutes.
  • On the µC side, RIN (e.g. 4.7 kΩ) and RIS_PROT limit the current so reverse battery and loss of ground do not push large currents into µC pins.

Inductive loads and SMART CLAMPING

When an inductive load (solenoid valve, relay coil, motor) is switched off, the inductance keeps the current flowing and drags OUT below ground. With nothing to catch it, the voltage keeps falling until the device breaks down. SMART CLAMPING holds VDS at VDS(CL) (35 V typ. at 25 °C), so OUT stops at VS − VDS(CL) ≈ 13.5 − 35 = −21.5 V and the MOSFET absorbs the inductor's energy in its active region. Ignoring load resistance (RL = 0, the conservative case):

E = ½ L IL2 × VDS(CL) / (VDS(CL) − VS)　e.g. 1 mH, 19 A → ½ × 1 m × 361 × 35 / 21.5 ≈ 294 mJ

294 mJ is below the single-pulse limit EAS of 600 mJ but above the repetitive limit EAR of 130 mJ (19 A). So a 1 mH, 19 A solenoid can be switched off like this once in a while, but doing it every time accumulates damage; working backwards, the repetitive limit is about 0.44 mH. Beyond that, add a freewheeling diode or TVS, though a freewheeling diode makes the current decay slower (the solenoid releases later), which is a system trade-off. The clamp voltage rises with temperature and current by design, to increase energy capability.

Capacitive loads, lamps and heaters

  • Capacitive loads: the input capacitance of a downstream ECU charges at turn-on with a current of about C × dV/dt. If that plus the load current exceeds ICL(0), the switch trips as soon as it turns on. Rough estimate: worst case (1 V/µs, 65 A) only about 65 µF; typical (0.25 V/µs, 82 A) about 330 µF. The datasheet slopes are measured with a resistive load; measure capacitive loads.
  • Low battery + large capacitance: during cranking the battery-line resistance pulls VS down toward 4 V, charging takes longer and VDS is large, which can trigger PSD.
  • Lamps and PTC heaters: low cold resistance means a start-up current several times the steady state (about 10× for a tungsten lamp). Make sure the inrush peak stays below the minimum ICL(0), and include start-up heating in the thermal design.
  • Glow plugs, fans, pumps: typical applications listed in the datasheet; motor start and stall currents must also be checked against ICL(0) and thermal shutdown.

PWM and losses

The switching slope is deliberately slow (0.25 V/µs typ., rise time about 50 µs) to keep EMI low (see Applications · EMI). The price is a lot of energy per switching event: at 13.5 V with a 0.68 Ω load, EON is about 5.5 mJ and EOFF about 4 mJ. The datasheet's total-loss equation:

P = (EON + EOFF + IL2 RDS(ON) tDC) / T　e.g. 100 Hz, 50%, 20 A → 0.95 W switching + about 0.8 W conduction

Hence the 200 Hz PWM limit: this part is for slow loads such as heaters and fans. kHz-class PWM (LED dimming, motor drives) needs a different part.

Thermal design and layout

At 24 A DC and 150 °C, RDS(ON) is 4.4 mΩ typ.: P ≈ 24² × 4.4 m ≈ 2.5 W, and with RthJA = 22 K/W on a 2s2p board the rise is about 56 K, so at 85 °C ambient TJ ≈ 141 °C; with the 5 mΩ maximum it is about 148 °C, right at the 150 °C minimum thermal-shutdown threshold. That is where IL(NOM) = 24 A comes from. On a single-layer board (35 K/W) the same current overheats.

  • The tab is VS: a large copper area with thermal vias to inner layers is the main heat path (see Applications · thermal for the method).
  • Tie both OUT pins together with symmetrical traces, sized for the maximum and short-circuit currents.
  • Place 100 nF close to VS (better EMC) and 10 nF on IS for filtering.
  • The dynamic rise ΔTJ during switching must stay below 60 K; in PWM, account for the temperature swing of each pulse.

General layout rules (high-current loops, Kelvin connections) are in Applications · layout.

Production test and qualification

Testing these parts resembles the LDO testing in the Test chapter, but with currents two orders of magnitude higher:

  • RDS(ON): 2–5 mΩ at 20 A is only a 50–100 mV drop. It needs Kelvin four-wire measurement, and probe and socket contact resistance are in the same range as the device; short current pulses avoid self-heating shifting the reading.
  • Sense-ratio trim: dkILIS is measured at two current points during test and trimmed if needed (the same idea as CP trim); the datasheet defines dkILIS by two points, 0.2 A and 60 A.
  • Overcurrent trip: the 65–100 A trip level has to be verified on the ATE with high-current pulses, a challenge for both the tester's current source and the contacts.
  • Clamp voltage: VDS(CL) is measured at 10 mA; energy capability (EAS, EAR) is a qualification item, not tested on every part.
  • "Specified by design": parameters marked "Not subject to production test, specified by design" (e.g. typical / maximum TJ(TRIP), thermal resistance, some timings) are not measured on every unit; they rely on characterization and design margin.
  • Qualification: AEC-Q100, plus short-circuit endurance (AEC-Q100-012: repeated shorts under defined conditions, graded by cycles survived) and repetitive inductive switch-off combined with temperature cycling. See Test · qualification.

AppsA new downstream module, and the switch trips at every startAutomotive · 12 V distribution
Symptom
After a zone-controller revision on one model, 2% of cold starts report a short-circuit fault at turn-on; retests at room temperature are fine, and every returned switch passes test (NTF).
Analysis
Measuring the turn-on current in the car: the new downstream module's input capacitance grew from 47 µF to 220 µF, with a charging peak of about 60 A. At low temperature the switch's slope is faster, the lot's trip levels sit near 65 A, and with the load current added the threshold is crossed.
Root cause
The system change did not re-evaluate inrush current; margin had been calculated with the 82 A typical value.
Action
Pre-charge in the downstream module; the distribution spec redefines "maximum capacitive load" using the minimum ICL(0) and the fastest slope; software retries once after a delay on the first trip.
Lesson
Design protection thresholds with the minimum value; "every return passes" usually means the problem is in the system, not the chip (see Complaints).

Terms

Smart high-side switch
A power switch between the battery and the load, integrating drive, protection and diagnostics.
Latch
After a protective turn-off the output stays off until reset from outside.
Freewheeling diode
A diode giving inductor current a loop to circulate after the switch turns off.
Loss of ground
The fault state when a module's ground wire comes loose.
Inrush current
The current, far above steady state, when a load is first switched on.
Glow plug
A heater that warms a diesel engine's combustion chamber for cold starts.
Zonal architecture
Organizing a car's power distribution and control by physical zone.
