Chapter 4 · Production

Production: from wafers to reels of tape

A power IC often sells for cents to a few dollars, yet ships in hundreds of millions a year. Making money means high yield at every step, short test times and small variation; keeping customers confident means every unit is traceable and every change is announced. This chapter walks through volume production and the numbers product engineers (PE) watch every day.

The production flow

WAT: an electrical check-up before wafers ship

Wafer acceptance test (WAT, also called PCM) measures basic device parameters on test keys in the scribe lines: VT, IDSAT, sheet resistance, contact resistance, BJT β, LDMOS Rsp and breakdown, capacitance, oxide breakdown.

  • Acceptance: 5–9 sites per wafer; out-of-spec wafers are not shipped or are downgraded.
  • Link to product parameters: pre-trim bandgap distributions correlate strongly with BJT VBE and resistor sheet resistance; RDS(on) with LDMOS Rsp. PEs build WAT-to-CP correlation models for early warning.
  • Process drift: WAT inside spec but skewed (e.g. sheet resistance high by 2σ) can push trim codes to their limit and drop yield.

CP test and trim

Wafer test (CP, chip probing) contacts each die's pads with a probe card. Power-IC CP has two special jobs: trimming, and measuring high-current parameters despite probe contact resistance.

  1. Opens / shorts and leakage confirm probe contact and healthy ESD structures.
  2. Measure pre-trim parameters: bandgap voltage, oscillator frequency, bias current, current-limit threshold and so on.
  3. Compute trim codes: from the pre-trim and target values, by table or formula, for each fuse / OTP bit.
  4. Program: blow fuses or write OTP, and log the trim code.
  5. Verify after trim: remeasure against tight limits; unblown fuses or out-of-spec units after trim fail.
  6. Other parameters: IQ, UVLO thresholds, RDS(on) (low-current), logic function, OTP read-back.
  • The trim-code distribution is a key monitor: codes centred mean a healthy process; codes near either end mean the process has shifted and the trim range is running out.
  • Package-shift pre-compensation: if packaging shifts the average by +0.3%, set the CP trim target to −0.3%. This value must be recalibrated regularly from paired CP / FT data.
  • High-current limits: each probe needle carries only about 0.5–1 A, so a 3 A current limit needs paralleled needles or is left to FT; 0.1–1 Ω probe resistance also means CP RDS(on) must be Kelvin-measured.
  • Multi-site: testing 4–32 dies at once lowers cost; site-to-site correlation must be checked regularly.

FT final test

Final test (FT) after packaging runs on a handler and measures the guaranteed datasheet parameters. Unlike CP it can test high current, real switching and thermal behaviour, and catches packaging-induced problems.

  • Temperature: consumer parts often test at room temperature only, relying on design and characterisation for the full range; automotive usually requires cold (−40 °C), room and hot (≥ 125 °C), or at least two temperatures.
  • Test time: about 0.3–1 s for an LDO, 5–20 s for a large PMIC. At a few cents per second of test, this matters for low-priced parts, so test items and settling waits are tightly budgeted.
  • Functional test: a PMIC must go through power-up sequencing, I²C reads / writes, every rail on / off and voltage, and fault triggering and flags.
  • Post-package trim: OTP or zener trim at FT corrects package stress, but needs suitable high-voltage sources on the tester.

Yield

Total yield Y = Yfab × YCP × Yassy × YFT　　YCP ≈ e−A·D0 × Yparam
  • Defect yield: depends on die area and defect density. Power-IC dies are small (1–10 mm²), so defect yield is usually high.
  • Parametric yield: often the main loss — trim range too small, IQ high, UVLO thresholds out of spec.
  • Yield-loss Pareto: sort failing bins weekly; the top three usually account for 70–80% of loss and are where improvement starts.
  • Wafer maps: low-yield edges, rings, scratches or particular reticle fields point straight at process or probe-card problems.
  • Retest: contact-related false fails can be recovered by retest, but a high retest rate means fixing the probe card or socket first, or real failures get hidden.

  • Test limits vs spec limits: test limits are usually tighter than datasheet limits; the gap is the guard band, covering tester error, temperature differences and long-term drift.
  • SPC control charts: monitor each lot's mean and standard deviation; violating Western Electric rules (e.g. seven points in a row on one side) triggers the OCAP.
  • Non-normal distributions: post-trim distributions are often uniform or multimodal; applying Cpk directly misleads — use the actual out-of-spec rate or non-parametric methods.

PAT and zero defects

Automotive customers aim for < 1 DPPM, or zero defects. Spec limits alone are not enough, because a part that "passes spec but sits far from its lot" often carries a latent defect.

  • Part average testing (PAT): per AEC-Q001, dynamic limits are computed from the lot or wafer distribution (e.g. a robust μ ± 6σ); outliers are removed even if within spec.
  • Geographic screening: GDBN (good die in bad neighbourhood) removes good dies surrounded by failures; whole regions or low-yield wafers can also be removed.
  • IDDQ and leakage: slightly high leakage can indicate oxide or junction defects, an early-failure precursor.
  • The cost: PAT typically discards an extra 0.5–2% of good parts — one reason automotive chips cost more.

Traceability

The first question in any complaint is "which lot, which wafer, which position did this come from?" The trace chain is usually:

  • Package marking: part number + date code + lot code (small packages may only fit 3–4 characters).
  • On-chip ECID: lot / wafer / X-Y written into OTP at CP and readable later through the interface; common in PMICs and automotive parts.
  • Linked databases: WAT, CP, FT, trim codes, tester and probe-card IDs, assembly lots and material lots all keyed by lot number — essential for "same-lot risk assessment" during complaints.
  • Retention: automotive typically requires production data to be kept for 15 years or more.

PCN and change control

Any change that may affect form, fit, function, quality or reliability must be notified to customers in advance (per JESD46, usually at least 90 days), with qualification data for major changes so customers can evaluate or requalify.

Capacity, lead time and second sources

  • 8-inch fabs: a lot of power ICs still run on 8-inch BCD lines. In the 2020–2022 shortage, 8-inch capacity was one of the tightest bottlenecks and lead times stretched to 40–52 weeks or more.
  • Second source: large customers want the same part number with capacity at two fabs or two assembly sites; each must be qualified and shown to match.
  • Pin-compatible substitutes: during shortages customers switch to another vendor's pin-compatible part, but compensation, soft start, PG timing and stable capacitor range differ, so oscillation and start-up problems after the switch are common application-support work.
