Process: BCD puts three kinds of transistor on one die

A power IC does not need the smallest transistors; it needs the widest variety of devices: power switches that withstand 40 V, well-matched precision resistors and BJTs, CMOS for digital control, and fuses for trimming. A BCD (Bipolar-CMOS-DMOS) process integrates them all — and brings isolation, parasitic and stress problems with it.

What BCD is

In the mid-1980s ST (then SGS) introduced the first BCD process, putting bipolar transistors (analogue precision), CMOS (logic) and DMOS (power) on one wafer. Every foundry has since developed its own BCD platforms, shrinking from several µm to 0.18 µm, 0.13 µm, 90 nm and recently 55 / 40 nm, so PMICs can integrate more digital logic and memory.

How it differs from logic processes　Logic processes chase density and speed; BCD chases device variety, voltage capability and precision. A BCD platform may use 30–40 masks, more than a logic process at the same node; each extra device (HV PMOS, high-resistance poly, MIM capacitor) adds masks and cost, so products pick only the modules they need.

LDMOS: trading breakdown against on-resistance

The key LDMOS metric is specific on-resistance Rsp = RDS(on) × area (mΩ·mm²). A longer, lighter drift region raises breakdown but also resistance; the silicon limit is roughly Rsp ∝ BV2.5. RESURF, multiple field plates and super-junctions all push toward that limit.

  • Area is cost: in a 5 V PMIC the power switches often take 30–50% of the die. Cutting Rsp by 20% shrinks the switch by 20% at the same efficiency.
  • Switching loss: the figure of merit FOM = RDS(on) × Qg (or × Qgd). High-frequency bucks care about gate charge, not just on-resistance.
  • SOA: an LDO pass device sees high voltage and high current together (e.g. in a short), which can trigger second breakdown (snapback) of the parasitic NPN — the main risk when LDMOS operates in the linear region.
  • Hot-carrier degradation: the high field in the drift region drives carriers into the gate oxide, drifting RDS(on) and VT over time — a key LDMOS qualification item.

Isolation and parasitics

One P substrate carries power nodes switching to VIN, analogue circuits with tens-of-µV precision and GHz logic. They must be kept apart.

Substrate injection: a side effect of the switch node

During the buck dead time the inductor current freewheels through the low-side body diode, and SW drops to about −0.7 V. The N-type drain is then forward-biased to the P substrate and injects a large number of minority carriers (electrons), which diffuse hundreds of µm and are collected by nearby N-wells as spurious current.

  • Symptoms: the bandgap jumps at switching edges, comparators false-trigger, ADC readings show errors synchronous with switching, and in bad cases latch-up.
  • Fixes: N+ / P+ double guard rings around power devices to collect carriers, shorter dead time, more distance between sensitive circuits and the power stage, DTI or SOI, and separate power and analogue grounds joined at one point.
  • The high side too: when SW rises above VIN, P regions forward-bias to the N-well and inject holes.
Why it shows up in complaints　Substrate injection depends on inductor current, dead time and temperature. Standard lab conditions may not show it; it appears when a customer uses more load current or longer trace inductance. It typically surfaces as "random resets" or "bad ADC readings" — see Troubleshooting · design issues.

On-chip compensation capacitors are typically only tens of pF (area-limited), so the big output capacitor is always off-chip. That is why LDO stability depends so heavily on the COUT the customer chooses.

Thick metal, on-resistance and electromigration

In a 3 A buck with a 40 mΩ high side, on-chip metal and package bonding can add another 10–30 mΩ. Power processes therefore offer 3–6 µm thick top metal, or even a copper RDL over the wafer to carry current straight from the power device to bumps or pads.

  • Electromigration (EM): at high current density, electrons push metal atoms along, forming voids or hillocks. Every metal segment and via must be checked against current-density limits at temperature; the allowance is much lower at 125–150 °C.
  • Bond-over-active: putting pads directly over active devices saves area, but bonding stress can crack the dielectric or shift device parameters underneath; it needs process qualification.
  • Budgeting the power-path resistance: when measuring RDS(on), separate silicon, metal, bond wire / bump and lead frame, or test values will not match the datasheet (see Test · Kelvin sensing).

Trimming: fuses, OTP and MTP

Process variation can leave a bandgap with ±1–2% initial error and an oscillator ±10–20% off, while products need ±0.5–1% and ±2–3%. The answer is to measure, compute and then program a trim code at wafer test (CP) or after packaging (FT).

Why post-package trim matters　Mould compound shrinks as it cures and puts tens of MPa of stress on the die; through the piezoresistive effect this shifts resistor values and BJT VBE, moving the bandgap by 0.1–0.5%. The tighter the accuracy, the more a product leans toward a final trim at FT, or at least toward pre-compensating the average shift during CP trim.

Matching and stress

LDO accuracy comes mainly from offset in the differential pair and current mirrors, the divider ratio and the BJT area ratio in the bandgap — all questions of how identical two "identical" devices really are.

σ(ΔVT) = AVT / √(W·L)　　σ(ΔR/R) = AR / √(W·L)
  • Pelgrom's law: mismatch scales with the inverse square root of area. Halving offset takes four times the area.
  • Common centroid, interdigitation and dummy devices cancel linear gradients and edge effects.
  • Keep away from stress sources: no precision devices in die corners, under pads or next to power devices (thermal gradients); orient resistors with the piezoresistive coefficients of the crystal in mind.
  • Chopping and auto-zero: move offset to high frequency at circuit level and filter it out — used in precision references and current sensing.

Process trends

  • Finer BCD nodes: phone and DDR5 PMICs integrate lots of digital control, OTP and ADCs, pushing BCD to 55 / 40 nm while keeping 5 V LDMOS.
  • GaN and SiC: 48 V server power, on-board chargers and fast chargers are adopting GaN HEMT switches with an order-of-magnitude better speed and FOM; control stays in silicon, as multi-die or monolithic GaN integration.
  • Integrated passives: silicon (deep-trench) capacitors and in-package inductors (PwrSiP, IVR) bring power closer to the processor and lower power-delivery impedance.
  • Vertical power delivery: AI accelerators draw over a thousand amps, so power modules move from beside the processor to directly beneath it, shortening the high-current path.

Glossary

BCD
Bipolar-CMOS-DMOS integrated process.
LDMOS
Laterally diffused MOS, the workhorse power device of power ICs.
Rsp
Specific on-resistance, RDS(on) × area.
RESURF
Reduced surface field: doping and field plates that spread the drift-region field evenly to raise breakdown.
NBL
N-type buried layer, used for junction isolation and to lower parasitic PNP gain.
DTI
Deep trench isolation.
Guard ring
A ring around a device that collects injected carriers and lowers latch-up risk.
OTP
One-time programmable non-volatile memory.
Piezoresistive effect
Stress changing semiconductor resistance and carrier mobility.
SOA
Safe operating area: the allowed combinations of voltage, current and time.
FOM
Figure of merit, e.g. RDS(on)·Qg, comparing switching devices.
