Chapter 1 · Architecture

Architecture: turning one voltage into another

A battery gives 3.0–4.4 V, USB 5–20 V, a car battery 9–16 V (dropping to 3 V in a cold crank and jumping above 40 V in a load dump). Yet an SoC core wants 0.6–1.0 V, DDR 1.1 V and analogue circuits a clean 1.8 V or 3.3 V. A power IC's job is to turn the input into exactly the voltage each load needs, across every input and load condition.

Why regulate at all

Load current can jump from microamps to amps in nanoseconds; the input moves as the battery discharges, a charger is plugged in or an engine cranks. A regulator is a negative-feedback system: it measures the output, compares it with a reference and adjusts the pass element so the output stays put. Judging a regulator means asking how far the output moves under these disturbances, how quickly it comes back and how much energy that costs.

Linear vs switching regulation

A linear regulator runs its pass element in the saturation / linear region, acting as a variable resistor controlled by feedback. The excess voltage (VIN − VOUT) times the current all becomes heat. It is simple, quiet and fast, and needs no inductor.

A switching regulator keeps its switches either fully on or fully off, so ideally they dissipate nothing, then filters the pulses into DC with an inductor and capacitor. It is efficient, but has switching ripple, EMI and more external parts.

ηLDO = (VOUT·IOUT) / (VIN·(IOUT + IQ)) ≈ VOUT / VIN

Dropping 3.6 V to 1.8 V, an LDO is at most 50% efficient; to 0.8 V, only 22%. So high-current rails with large headroom use DC-DC converters, while clean rails, small headroom or small currents use LDOs. The most common combination is a DC-DC that steps close to the target, followed by an LDO as a filter (post-regulation).

The LDO block diagram

  • Reference: usually a bandgap with a tempco below 50 ppm/°C; it sets the output accuracy.
  • Error amplifier: its gain sets regulation, its bandwidth sets transient response, and its output swing decides whether the pass device can be fully turned on (which affects dropout).
  • Pass device: a large PMOS or NMOS that may take over half the LDO's area and has the largest gate capacitance.
  • Feedback divider: sets the output voltage; built in for fixed-output parts, brought out to an FB pin on adjustable parts.
  • Protection: current limit, thermal shutdown, undervoltage lockout (UVLO), soft start and active discharge — see Design · protection.

Dropout, efficiency and power dissipation

Dropout voltage (VDO) is the minimum VIN − VOUT at which the output still holds its setpoint, usually defined where the output has fallen 1–2% or 100 mV. In dropout, a PMOS LDO's pass device is fully on and behaves as a resistor:

VDO ≈ IOUT · RDS(on)　　PD = (VIN − VOUT)·IOUT + VIN·IQ　　TJ = TA + PD·θJA

Example: 5 V to 3.3 V at 500 mA gives PD = 0.85 W. A SOT-23 with θJA ≈ 200 °C/W would rise 170 °C and certainly shut down; a DFN with θJA ≈ 45 °C/W and copper for heat-sinking works. When choosing an LDO, heat often limits you before the current rating does.

  • PSRR collapses in dropout: the pass device enters the linear region, gain disappears and input ripple passes straight through. Datasheet PSRR is usually measured with VIN − VOUT ≥ 0.5–1 V.
  • Dropout rises with temperature: RDS(on) has a tempco of about +0.3–0.5 %/°C, so dropout is 30–50% higher when hot.
  • End of battery life: as the battery approaches VOUT, the LDO is fully on; the smaller the dropout, the more battery capacity is usable.

Key LDO specifications

Output accuracy
The sum of reference, divider, EA offset, temperature, line and load regulation; typically ±1% (25 °C) or ±2% (full temperature).
Quiescent current IQ
Current the LDO draws itself at no load. Wearables and IoT want < 1 µA; high-PSRR, high-bandwidth LDOs may need tens to hundreds of µA.
Ground current IGND
Current to ground under load; close to IQ for PMOS LDOs, rising with load for PNP LDOs.
Shutdown current ISD
Leakage with EN low; sets standby battery life, typically < 100 nA.
PSRR
How much input ripple is rejected, in dB, falling with frequency. See Design · PSRR.
Output noise
Integrated noise from 10 Hz to 100 kHz, in µVRMS. Low-noise LDOs reach < 5 µVRMS.
Stable capacitor range
The minimum COUT and ESR range in the datasheet. Outside it the LDO may oscillate — the most common application problem.
Current limit / short-circuit current
Maximum current into a short; may be constant-current or foldback.

The buck converter

D = VOUT/VIN (ideal)　ΔIL = VOUT(1 − D) / (L·fSW)　ΔVOUT ≈ ΔIL / (8·fSW·COUT) + ΔIL·ESR
  • Synchronous vs asynchronous: a low-side MOSFET (synchronous) replaces the Schottky diode and saves its 0.3–0.5 V drop, which matters a lot at low output voltages. The cost is a dead time to keep both switches from conducting at once (shoot-through).
  • CCM / DCM: if inductor current never reaches zero it is continuous conduction (CCM); at light load it drops to zero (DCM). A synchronous buck forced into CCM (FPWM) at light load sees current reverse — inefficient but with fixed ripple.
  • Light-load modes: PFM / pulse skipping reduce switching at light load and lift efficiency from 50% to over 85%, at the cost of larger ripple and a variable frequency that may land in audio or RF-sensitive bands.
  • Switching frequency: higher frequency means smaller inductors and capacitors, but more switching and drive loss. Phone PMICs commonly run 1.5–4 MHz; automotive parts avoid the AM band (530–1700 kHz) with 400 kHz or 2.1 MHz.

Charge pumps (switched capacitor)

A charge pump converts voltage with switches and capacitors only — no inductor. The ratio is fixed (×2, ×1.5, ÷2, −1) and efficiency is high when the ratio is "just right"; otherwise the excess voltage is lost much as in an LDO.

  • 2:1 divider: phone fast chargers halve a USB input set to twice the battery voltage, reaching 97–98% efficiency and far less heat than a buck charger.
  • Doublers and inverters: gate drive for NMOS pass devices or high-side switches, LCD bias, RS-232 interfaces.
  • Hybrids: a charge pump plus an inductor (3-level buck, Dickson + inductor) for 48 V-to-low-voltage server power, lowering switch voltage ratings and inductor size.

PMIC: the whole power system in one chip

A PMIC integrates multiple bucks, LDOs, boosts, load switches, power sequencing, monitoring ADCs, watchdogs, RTC, chargers and digital interfaces. It saves area and puts sequencing and monitoring in one place; the challenge is that switching converters and sensitive analogue share one substrate, so thermal and noise coupling — and test complexity — grow sharply.

  • DVS / AVS: the processor adjusts its core voltage with load and frequency; the PMIC must slew at a set rate within microseconds on command (SPMI, I²C, PMBus, SVID / SVI3).
  • Multiphase buck: high-current core rails (10–100 A and beyond) interleave phases to cut ripple and spread heat, and can drop phases at light load (phase shedding).
  • OTP configuration: one die becomes many part numbers via OTP settings for voltage, sequencing and protection thresholds. Wrong OTP programming is a common source of complaints (see Complaints · cases).
